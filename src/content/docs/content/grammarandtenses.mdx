The rules for constructing correct, consistent sentences. Grammar and tense guide sentence mechanics and structure.

## Active voice and tense

Most government writing defaults to passive voice. It's one of the biggest barriers between people and the information they need. Passive voice hides who is responsible for an action, making content feel bureaucratic, evasive, and harder to follow. Active voice makes clear who does what.
- Use present tense and active voice by default
- Future tense is appropriate when describing something that genuinely happens later: "This name will be registered with the State only after you submit the rest of this form."
- Passive voice is acceptable when the actor is genuinely unknown or unimportant: "Forms issued by the Office of Government Ethics include the OGE-450."
- Use tools like [Grammarly](https://www.grammarly.com/) or [Tense checker](https://www.tensechecker.com/) to check if your content is active or passive voice

| ✅ Do | ❌  Don’t |
| ----------- | ----------- |
| You need a certificate | A certificate is needed from you |
| The State will review your application within 10 business days | Your application will be reviewed within 10 business days |
| Enter your Social Security number | Your Social Security number should be entered |

## Contractions 

Contractions make content feel human and conversational, and plain language standards encourage them. They're a simple tool for closing the distance between an agency and the people it serves. But they require judgment, for two reasons:

**Accessibility.** Research from the UK's Government Digital Service found that some users with disabilities struggle to understand “can't" as "cannot.” For users with limited English proficiency, lower literacy, or cognitive disabilities, spelling out negatives removes that risk.

**Translation.** Most languages don't use contractions the way English does. Contracted source text creates extra work for human translators and can introduce ambiguity in machine translation. If your content will be translated, spelling out all contractions gives translators cleaner source text to work from.

Use your best judgement when writing contractions. Use common and recognized words, and avoid writing negative contractions:
- Use positive contractions (you’ll, we’re, it’s) when it sounds natural
- Use commonly recognized contractions (we’ll, you’re, it’s, they’re, etc.)
- Never use ambiguous or awkward contractions, like “sources’ll,” “there’d,” “results’re”
- Always spell out “of” contractions (could have, would have, should have, etc.)
- Always spell out negative contractions (cannot, will not, do not)
- Use “it’s” when you mean “it is” or “it has.” Use “its” to show possession 
- Don’t mix contractions and their spelled-out equivalents (for example, don’t use “can’t” and “cannot” in the same UI) 

### Positive and commonly recognized contractions 

| ✅ Do | ❌  Don’t |
| ----------- | ----------- |
| You’ll need these documents to apply. | You will need these documents to apply. |
| We’ll contact you about your application. | We will contact you about your application. |
| The results are useless. | The results’re useless. |


### Negative contractions 

| ✅ Do | ❌  Don’t |
| ----------- | ----------- |
| We could not save your progress. | We couldn’t save your progress. |
| Do not include dashes in your Social Security number. | Don’t include dashes in your Social Security number. |

## Parallel construction 
Each item should start with the same part of speech: use the same voice, the same verb tense, and the same sentence type. This helps users scan and process content quickly.

| ✅ Do | ❌  Don’t |
| ----------- | ----------- |
| ... | ... |
| ... | ... |


## Which vs. that 
Use “that” when the clause is essential to the meaning, and removing it would change what the sentence says:
- ✓ Correct: "Identify a start date that considers potential delays."

Use “which” (preceded by a comma) for non-essential information:
- ✓ Correct: "The taxes are paid on a company's taxable income, which is revenue minus the cost of goods sold."

**A useful test:** if you can remove the clause without losing the core meaning, use "which." If removing it breaks the sentence, use "that."

## Fewer vs. less
Use “less than” or “less,” not “fewer” or “fewer than.”
- ✓ Correct: "fewer than 30 days"
- ✓ Correct: "fewer than 5 employees"
- ✓ Correct: "fewer than 7 items in the list"
- ✓ Correct: "less time"
- ✓ Correct: "less effort"
- ✓ Correct: "less paperwork”
