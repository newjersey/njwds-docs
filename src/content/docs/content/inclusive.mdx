We write with a person-first perspective. Whether you're writing for residents, business owners, or
internal teams, use language that is compassionate, inclusive, and respectful. The words we choose
reflect our values and shape how people experience government.

A few principles to guide inclusive writing:

- Write about people, not data points or categories. Every person reading our content has a real
  situation with real stakes.
- Don't reference personal characteristics unless they're directly relevant to the content. This
  includes characteristics like age, disability, medical condition, race, gender.
- When in doubt, use the most specific, respectful language available. Always follow the person's
  own preference when you know it.

The guidance in this section was based on the
[APA Inclusive Language Guide](https://www.apa.org/about/apa/equity-diversity-inclusion/language-guidelines)
and the [Conscious Style Guide](https://consciousstyleguide.com/).

## Specific guidance

### Age

- Don't reference a person's age unless it's relevant. If it is relevant, include the specific age:
  Example: "Applicants must be 60+ years old to be eligible for this benefit.”
- Use "older person" or "senior." Do not use "elderly," "old," or age-related descriptors that carry
  negative connotations.
- Avoid describing people as "young" in ways that diminish their credibility or authority.

### Community language

- Use "underserved community" not "disadvantaged community"

### Disability

- Never assume someone has a mental, medical, or cognitive condition. Don’t refer to a person’s
  medical condition unless it’s relevant to what you’re writing.
- Use person-first language by default: "a person with a disability," not "a disabled person" —
  unless the individual or community prefers identity-first language ("they are disabled"), in which
  case follow their preference. Don’t describe someone as “mentally ill.”
- Don’t refer to someone’s mental or cognitive condition unless it’s relevant to what you’re
  writing.
- Don't use the words "suffers from," "victim of," or "handicapped" when writing about a person with
  a disability. ("Handicapped parking" is an established term and is acceptable.)
- Avoid disability-related idioms: not "lame," "falling on deaf ears," "blind to the issue," or
  similar phrases.
- For hearing: use "deaf" as an adjective for significant hearing loss; "hard of hearing" or
  "partially deaf" are also appropriate.
- For vision: use "blind" to describe a person unable to see; use "low vision" for limited vision.

### Family relationships

Use broad, neutral terms:

- Spouse or partner (not husband or wife)
- Child or dependent child (not son or daughter)
- Stepchild (not stepson or stepdaughter)
- Parent (not mother or father)

In some cases, form fields or backend data may require gendered terms. Follow the system constraint
and flag for future remediation.

### Gender

- Use "they" and "their" as the default singular pronoun. Never "he or she" or "his or her."
- When writing about a specific person, use their communicated pronouns. When in doubt, use their
  name.
- Use gender-neutral occupational titles: firefighter not fireman; chairperson not chairman; server
  not waitress; businessperson not businessman.
- Don't call groups of people "guys." Don't refer to women as "girls."
- Use the following words only as modifiers, never as nouns: lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender
  (never "transgendered"), trans, queer, LGBT.
  - ✓ Correct: "The program serves transgender residents" (modifier)
  - ✗ Incorrect: "The program serves transgenders" (noun)
  - ✓ Correct: "LGBT-inclusive policies apply statewide" (modifier)
  - ✗ Incorrect: "This policy applies to LGBTs" (noun)
- Don't use "same-sex marriage" unless the legal distinction is specifically relevant. Otherwise,
  it's just "marriage."
- Avoid stereotypes or appearance-based descriptions unless directly relevant to the content.

### Indigenous and tribal communities

- Use terms approved by the appropriate authority (OPA or equivalent)
- When referring to a specific tribe, use the tribe's full official name
- If uncertain, follow the tribe's own naming preferences

### Nationality and citizenship

- Avoid "citizen" as a general term; use "people," "the public," or "users.” Use "citizen" only when
  referring to legal U.S. citizenship.
- Be thoughtful with "Americans" or "the American public" — these can exclude non-citizens. Use "the
  public" for inclusive contexts.

### New Jersey residency

- Avoid the word "resident” – it can be confused with its legal definition. Instead use "people who
  live in New Jersey" or a project-specific term (for example, "doulas from New Jersey").
