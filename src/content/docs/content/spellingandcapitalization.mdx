How to write individual words, abbreviations, acronyms, and numbers.

## Acronyms, abbreviations, and initialisms

### Initialisms

Initialisms are abbreviations pronounced letter by letter rather than as a word. FBI is an initialism because you say "F-B-I." NASA is an acronym because you say "NASA." 

The article (“a,” “an,” “the”) before an initialism [depends on the initialism’s sound](https://our-languages.canada.ca/en/writing-tips-plus/abbreviations-acronyms-and-initialisms), not its spelling. Use “an” before initialisms that begin with a vowel sound when spoken aloud, even if the first letter is a consonant:
- an FAQ (sounds like "an eff-ay-kyoo")
- an NPI number (sounds like "an en-pea-eye")
- an HTML form (sounds like "an aitch-tee-em-ell")
- an MRI (sounds like "an em-are-eye")

Use “a” before initialisms that begin with a consonant sound:
- a URL (sounds like "a you-are-ell") 
- a PDF (sounds like "a pea-dee-eff")

When in doubt, say it out loud. The spoken sound determines the article.

### Acronyms and abbreviations

Acronyms are common in government writing, but overusing them creates barriers. The more acronyms a document contains, the harder it becomes to follow and understand. This negatively impacts users who are new to a topic, have lower literacy, or are reading in a second language. 

Whenever possible, you should use nicknames to refer to organizations and legislation. Simplified names give readers meaningful context that helps them remember what you’re talking about. 

Use acronyms when:
- They genuinely help your reader
- The term is widely recognized
- The term repeats frequently enough that repeating the full name would be more burdensome than helpful

Follow these guidelines when writing acronyms:
- Some acronyms are more recognizable than their full names (NASA, FBI, ZIP code). In those cases, use the acronym without spelling it first.
- Limit the number of acronyms and abbreviations you use. Use no more than three acronyms in one document.
- [Spell out the full term on first use](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Techniques/general/G97), followed by the acronym in parentheses: Internal Revenue Service (IRS).
  - First use: International Revenue Service (IRS)
  - Second use: IRS
- Only include the acronym in parentheses if you will use it again.
- Always keep a context word with the acronym: say "FFS application," not just "FFS."
- Do not capitalize acronyms or initialisms when spelled out, unless they are a proper noun.
- Acronyms are the only words written in all caps.
- If abbreviating a term that’s possessive, don’t include the possessive s. 
  - ✓ The Department of Education’s (DOE) student loan program.
  - ✗ The Department of Education's (DOE’s) student loan program.

If you’re having trouble, check the [Federal Plain Language Guidelines](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language), [AP stylebook](https://www.apstylebook.com/) and [W3C: Web Accessibility Initiative](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/abbreviations.html). 

You can also ask whether the spelled-out term refers to a specific, named entity (an agency, a law, a program). If yes, capitalize it as you would any proper noun. If it's a generic description of a concept or technology, keep it lowercase.

| ✅ Do capitalize when spelled out because it’s a proper noun | ❌  Don’t capitalize when spelled out because it’s not a proper noun |
| ----------- | ----------- |
| IRS > Internal Revenue Service | HTML > hypertext markup language |
| SSA > Social Security Administration | PDF > portable document format |

## Capitalization 

### Use sentence case 

Capitalization conventions vary by language. While English defaults to sentence case for headings and interface elements, Spanish and other languages follow different grammatical rules. Spanish headings, for example, typically use title cases. Do not apply English capitalization rules to translated content. Refer to the [NJ Translation and Localization Guide](https://docs.google.com/document/d/12bUhW4SdpTCflhQP-KeRFAy2lNXuP5KfJU7ZwoGRVNg/edit?disco=AAACDm6v9PI&tab=t.tps2rfoz4x3c) for guidance.

When writing English copy, capitalize only the first word and proper nouns for all of the following:
- Page titles and headings (H1–H6)
- Subheadings
- Buttons and calls to action
- Alerts and banners
- Labels, tabs, and system navigation
- Form field labels

This is consistent with [VA.gov](http://VA.gov), [GSA.gov](http://GSA.gov), the [U.S. Web Design System (USWDS)](https://designsystem.digital.gov/components/list/), and many other federal institutions. [Research by the USAGov content team](https://www.usa.gov/blog/2023/09/making-the-case-for-sentence-case) found sentence case easier for content designers to apply consistently, better suited to mobile displays (reduces line wrapping), and aligned with modern conversational government style. All without any measurable loss of user trust.

| ✅ Do | ❌  Don’t |
| ----------- | ----------- |
| Your timeline for action | Your Timeline for Action |
| Save and continue | Save and Continue |
| Apply now for GI Bill benefits | Apply Now For GI Bill Benefits |

### Always capitalize 

- Proper nouns: names of people, places, agencies, products, days of the week and months, and titles of books
- Formal titles of forms: Form 1040, U.S. Individual Income Tax Return.
- Personal titles when they precede a name: “Director López got approval”
- The first word of any sentence, bullet point, or heading
- The first word after a colon only if it begins a complete sentence
- NJ departments and agencies: follow naming and capitalization at [NJ.gov's Department and Agency page](http://NJ.gov), including whether they use "and" or "&"

### Capitalizing "State" 

#### Do not capitalize “state” unless it’s part of a proper name. Keep “state” lowercase if it describes a type of object, tier, or setting (state laws, state troopers, state funded, etc.)

| ✅ Do | ❌  Don’t | ❓ Why |
| ----------- | ----------- | ----------- |
| DOL administered the survey at the state level | DOL administered the survey at the State level | In this sentence, "state" is a common descriptor modifying the noun "level." It specifies the geographic or administrative scale of the survey (as opposed to the national level or local level). Because it refers to a generic tier of government and does not name a specific state, it is a common adjective. |
| Funding cutbacks have heavily impacted state universities | Funding cutbacks have heavily impacted State universities | The term "state universities" is a generic classification for a type of institution (universities funded by public, state money). You only capitalize "State" here if it is part of the official, legal name of a specific institution. For instance, Ohio State University or San Diego State University. When you are talking about state universities as a broad group, "state" is just a generic descriptor and must stay lowercase. |
| The state of New Jersey | The State of New Jersey | In this example, “state” is a common noun describing the type of entity, while “New Jersey” is the proper noun. 
You wouldn’t capitalize “the city of Chicago” or “the continent of Africa” because the generic descriptor doesn't become part of the official name. |

#### Capitalize "State" when it’s a proper noun, official name, or entity:
- As part of the full name (State of New Jersey)
- When the word it modifies is capitalized (the State Commissioner)
- When referring to the state as an actor (the State filed; doing business with the State)
- When it’s part of a proper name (I loved visiting New Jersey State)

| ✅ Do | ❌  Don’t | ❓ Why |
| ----------- | ----------- | ----------- |
| The funding was appropriated directly by the State of New Jersey | The funding was appropriated by the state of New Jersey | Capitalizing "State" here indicates you are referring to the specific legislative and governing entity that holds the legal authority to appropriate public funds. |
| The guidelines were approved by the State Commissioner of Education | The guidelines were approved by the state Commissioner of Education | Always capitalize official titles when they serve as a formal name or part of an official department heading. If you wrote, "She is a state commissioner," it would be lowercase because it is a generic description of a job role. |
| The defense argued that the State filed the lawsuit without sufficient evidence. | The defense argued that the state filed the lawsuit without sufficient evidence. | In legal documents, academic case reviews, and court-related context, "the State" replaces the specific name of a person or entity acting in a governmental capacity. Because it functions as a proper name for a specific party in a lawsuit, it must be capitalized. |
| While I have toured New York City, I spent most of my summer visiting New Jersey State. | While I have toured New York City, I spent most of my summer visiting New Jersey state. | "State" is used here as an essential part of a proper geographic name to distinguish it from a city or regional counterpart. |

### Capitalization exceptions 
We use sentence case capitalization as the standard across our products, but there are a few exceptions:
- "Veteran" and "Veterans" ([per federal standards](https://design.va.gov/content-style-guide/capitalization#:~:text=Proper%20nouns%20(such%20as%20branded,and%20Find%20a%20VA%20Location))) 

### Do not capitalize
- Generic references to forms or processes: income taxes, income tax forms
- Words like agile, open source, federal, government, unless it’s the first word of a sentence
- Acronyms or initialisms when spelled out, unless they are a proper noun ("x-ray,” for example, is always lowercase)
- Email addresses and URLs. These are always lowercase: myemail@nj.gov
- Licenses and permits in body copy unless part of an official title: mercantile license vs. Mercantile License Fee Survey


## Dates, times, and addresses

### Dates
- Spell out the full date: January 15, 2025 (not January 15th, 2025)
- Use cardinal numbers, not ordinals: January 5 not January 5th
- Add a comma after the year when the date appears mid-sentence: "The deadline is January 15, 2025, for all applicants."
- Spell out days of the week and months in body copy
- For date ranges, use "from/to," "from/through," or "between/and" intentionally — each implies something slightly different about whether the end date is included
- Abbreviate months only when space is very limited (callout boxes, alerts): Jan., Feb., Aug., Sept., Oct., Nov., Dec. Do not abbreviate March, April, May, June, or July.

### Times 
- Write out times using a.m. and p.m. with periods: 9:00 a.m.
- Always include minutes, even on the hour: 9:00 a.m., not 9 a.m.
- Spell out noon and midnight. Do not use 12:00 p.m. or 12:00 a.m.
- For time ranges: 8:00 a.m. to 5:30 p.m. ET
- Always include days and hours of operation when listing a contact number

### Phone numbers 
- Write the phone number with digits, not letters. Use hyphens between numbers, and don’t use parenthesis for the area code: 123-455-6565
- Do not use vanity phone numbers with letters instead of digits, like “1-800-NJ-TAXES”
- Include days and hours of operation whenever listing a phone number
- For extensions: 202-123-1234, ext. 9
- Use "call" before a phone number and "text" before a text number
- Hyperlink all phone numbers for accessibility. Don’t link the “call” or “text” verb before the number
- Include the TTY number directly after the phone number: 866-440-1238 (TTY: 711)
- Use "select" (not "press") when referencing phone menu options: "Call 988 and select 1"

### Addresses 
- Spell out street names in body copy and do not abbreviate: Street, Road, Avenue, Boulevard, Highway
- Use title case for address blocks
- Do not use periods in PO Box: PO Box 45005, not P.O. Box 45005
- Always spell out state names in body copy. Use postal abbreviations only in formatted address blocks with ZIP codes
- "ZIP code" for U.S. addresses; "postal code" for international or unknown addresses
- Do not use ZIP+4 codes in web content

### State names 
- Always spell out state names in body copy: Mississippi, not MS
- When listing a city and state together, spell out the state: Atlanta, Georgia

Always capitalize "State" when referring to a State as a proper noun. For more guidance, visit the State capitalization section.


## Etcetera (Etc.), for example (e.g.), and in other words (i.e.) 
- Don’t use “etc.” (etcetera) if you are starting a list with “for example.” You don’t need extra redundancy
  - ✓ Correct: There are many types of coffee (lattes, americano, cappuccino, etc.).
  - ✗ Incorrect: There are many types of coffee (e.g., glazed, powder, chocolate, etc.).
- Use "for example," not "e.g." or "i.e." 

## Numbers 

### Ordinals 
To [maximize scannability and accessibility](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/web-writing-show-numbers-as-numerals/), always use digits (1, 2, 3) instead of spelling out numbers (one, two, three), even for numbers under 10.
- ✓ Correct: "You must submit 2 forms of identification."
- ✗ Incorrect: "You must submit two forms of identification."

While traditional style manuals (like AP Style) suggest spelling out small numbers, digital government standards prioritize accessibility and scannability. Digits serve as immediate visual anchors for users scanning a screen under stress. Digits are also highly accessible for users with lower literacy levels, cognitive disabilities, or individuals using digital translation tools.

Only spell out a number under 10 if it’s part of a common phrase or proper noun ("the first step,” “one of the reasons,” “one-on-one,” etc.).
- If a sentence starts with a numeral, rewrite the sentence so the number isn't the first word. If rearranging isn’t possible, use the numeral (1, 2, 3, etc.)
- Spell out ordinals first through ninth (“first,” “second,” etc.), unless in a series or range (“steps 1-3”)
- Use numerals for 10th and above: 10th, 11th, 25th
- Do not use ordinals in dates: January 15 not January 15th
- A sequence of numbers should use the same format, which should follow the higher number (6th out of 12)
- Use commas to separate thousands in numbers of four digits or more (“1,000”), except when writing years
- Do not use a hyphen or en dash to indicate a range of numbers, separate with “to” (around 15 to 20 people)
- Spell out numbers that don’t represent specific facts
  - ✓ Correct: “...in recent years, we have tested thousands of users.” “Thousands,” in this case, isn’t referring to specific data

### Money
- Use the dollar sign ($) with no space before the number: $27, $19.99, $2.7 million, 5 cents. Follow the same format for other currencies
- In running text, omit ".00" for whole dollar amounts: $2,150
- In tables and lists, add ".00": $407.00
- Spell out large round amounts: $8 million not $8M


### Percentages
- Never spell out the word “percent”
- Use the % symbol with no space between the number and symbol: 10%


### Decimals 
- Use decimal points when a number can’t be easily written out as a fraction (47.2, 55.6, etc.)


### Fractions
- Avoid using fractions, whenever possible. Fractions are hard for users with low numeracy to understand, and can be hard for screen readers
- Use percentages to reflect fractions: 
  - ✓ Correct: 75% of the time
  - ✗ Incorrect: ¾ of the time


### Time
- Write out lengths of time in full. Be specific and descriptive
  - ✓ Correct: 5 hours and 15 minutes,
  - ✗ Incorrect: 5.25 hours 
  - ✓ Correct: months to 1 year
  - ✗ Incorrect: 6 to 12 months
- Use decimals only in tables where space is limited
- Write out times, using “a.m.” or “p.m,” with a space in between. Don’t use minutes for on-the-hour time: 9 a.m., 8:30 p.m. 
- Specify time zones when writing about an event or something to schedule. For time zones in the U.S., use abbreviations: Eastern time (ET), Central Time (CT), Mountain time (MT), Pacific time (PT)
- When referring to international time zones, spell them out. If a time zone doesn’t have a set name, use its Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) offset


### Decades
- Do not use an apostrophe with the "s" when referencing decades: the early 2000s, not the early 2000's.
- Always write out decades (the 1900s, the 2000s, the 1890s)


## Pronouns 
- Use "you" and "your" to address the user directly. 
- Use "we" and "our" to represent the agency. 
- Use "they" and "their" as the default singular pronoun — never "he or she" or "his or her."
- Avoid detached institutional language.

| ✅ Do | ❌  Don’t |
| ----------- | ----------- |
| Complete any combination of the following activities to keep receiving your health care coverage | Beneficiaries must complete a combination of approved community engagement activities to maintain eligibility. |
| If your provider has filled out the form electronically, they must print it out to sign it. | If your provider has filled out the form electronically, he or she must print it out to sign it. |
