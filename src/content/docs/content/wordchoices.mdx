The words we choose are the single biggest factor in whether someone understands us or doesn't. Government writing has a long history of favoring complex, formal language — often because it feels more authoritative. But clarity is authority. When people can understand us immediately, they trust us more, not less.

A few principles to guide every word choice:
- Use the familiar word over the far-fetched one, the concrete word over the abstraction, and the short word over the long one
- Avoid jargon, technical terms, and filler words when everyday words mean the same thing
- Avoid double negatives. They force readers to do extra mental work to extract the meaning
  - ✓ Correct: "You must submit the form on time"
  - ✗ Incorrect: "Failure to not submit the form late will not go unnoticed"
- Define terms only when necessary. If you must define something, place the definition where the reader needs it — not in a glossary they have to hunt through


## Writing in Plain Language
Plain language means a reader can find what they need, understand it the first time, and act on it, without having to read it twice or ask someone to translate it for them. We follow plain language principles for every piece of content we write. 


## Word list
Refer to the [AP style guide](https://www.apstylebook.com/) for terms not included in this table.

| ✅ Do | ❌  Don’t | ❓ Why |
| ----------- | ----------- | ----------- |
| Social Security number | Social Security Number | Only the proper noun gets capitalized. "Social Security" is the proper name of the federal program, but "number" is just a generic noun describing what it is. |
| Nonprofit | Non-profit | Style guides like AP have moved away from hyphenating common "non-" prefixed words once they're established in everyday language. |
| Health care | Healthcare | Styled as two words per AP Style, even though usage has trended toward closing this compound. Treated as an intentional exception since "health care" remains the more formal/traditional spelling and is still widely used in legal, government, and insurance contexts (e.g., "Affordable Care Act," "health care provider"). |
| On-site | Onsite | When "on-site" functions as a compound adjective before a noun (e.g., "on-site staff"), the hyphen prevents ambiguity and follows standard compound-modifier rules. "Onsite" as one word is a more casual/tech-industry shorthand. |
| EIN | FEIN | EIN (Employer Identification Number) is the IRS's actual official term. "FEIN" (Federal EIN) is an informal variant some states use, but it's not IRS terminology. |
